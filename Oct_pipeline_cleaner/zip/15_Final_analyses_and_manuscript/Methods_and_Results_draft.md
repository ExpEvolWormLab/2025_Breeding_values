## Methods

### Estimation of fitness-associated multivariate phenotypes

We analyzed seven phenotypic components measured in the high-salt environment in the CeMEE recombinant inbred lines (RILs): six behavioral traits (SF, SB, FS, FB, BS, and BF) and body area (area.F). For the present study, we estimated phenotypic selection gradients from a multiple regression of relative fitness on the seven standardized trait measurements summarized at the RIL level, using the phenotype-processing procedure described above. The resulting vector of gradients was used to construct an a priori fitness-related composite phenotype, defined as the weighted sum of the seven traits. The composite was constructed before examining evolutionary allele-frequency change. The weights used here are specific to the present RIL fitness regression; they should not be conflated with retrospective selection gradients derived from the evolutionary response and genetic covariance matrix in Mallard et al. (2023). We retained all seven estimated gradients in the full index regardless of their individual statistical significance. As a sensitivity analysis, we constructed a reduced index retaining only SF, SB, and area.F, whose estimated gradient directions had motivated the original biological hypothesis, and setting the other four coefficients to zero. A separate area.F-only predictor assessed the contribution of the sole trait with a clearly nonzero gradient in the present regression.

### Genome-wide association mapping and LD clusters

Mixed-model genome-wide association tests were performed for the seven component traits and the fitness-related composite, using the same RIL genotype/kinship alignment and leave-one-chromosome-out relatedness correction across phenotypes. Associated SNPs were grouped into connected components using a pairwise linkage-disequilibrium threshold of \(r^2\geq0.5\). Effect-allele phases within components were harmonized using the signs of signed LD before cluster genotypes and cluster-level effects were computed. This procedure produced 1,089 R5 QTL clusters. To compare evolutionary responses across traits on the same scale, we retained one allele-oriented effect estimate per cluster and phenotype, and reconstructed the full and reduced multivariate genetic-effect scores using the selection-gradient weights and the trait standardization factors used for GWAS. The mapped composite and the composite reconstructed from individual-trait GWAS coefficients were treated as related but distinct estimators.

### Evolutionary allele-frequency trajectories

Allele frequencies were estimated from pooled sequencing data at generation zero and at subsequent sampled generations during evolution in salt. The current analytical manifest includes four evolving sample trajectories, sampled at generations 10 and 36, with three additionally sampled at generation 50. **[AUTHOR CHECK: the manuscript draft describes three replicate populations; reconcile this discrepancy with the population metadata and distinguish experimental populations from sequenced samples.]** The original Pool-seq output was re-aligned with the explicit sample manifest so that sample labels and generation labels matched the frequency and depth matrices. The generation-zero sample with evidence of a bottleneck was excluded from the principal trajectory model; **[AUTHOR CHECK: confirm that the excluded sample was A6G140 and that A00 represents the appropriate common ancestor.]** The original two-baseline analysis was retained only as a sensitivity comparison.

For each ancestral polymorphism, counts were oriented to the GWAS alternative allele and a linear allele-frequency slope per generation was estimated using generalized least squares (GLS). Under the neutral covariance model, repeated observations from the same evolving population shared drift covariance determined by the smaller of their sampled generations and a diploid Wright–Fisher effective population size \(N_e\). Independent sequencing-read sampling variance was added to the covariance diagonal. We used \(N_e=1{,}000\) for the primary analysis and examined \(N_e=500\) and 2,000 in neutral calibration simulations. To stabilize frequency-dependent uncertainty, the null variance was evaluated using a GLS estimate of the shared allele frequency under the hypothesis of no time trend. The resulting slope is a **frequency change per generation**, not a population-genetic selection coefficient. Neutral Wright–Fisher trajectories were simulated using the observed sampling times and read depths; the same estimation procedure was applied to simulated and observed trajectories. Because extreme ancestral frequencies showed less reliable calibration, formal comparisons were restricted to SNPs with estimated ancestral allele frequencies from 0.10 to 0.90. The simulations were a calibration assessment of the assumed neutral model, not proof of calibration at arbitrarily small genome-wide p-values.

### Comparison of GWAS effects with evolutionary response

SNP-specific allele-frequency slopes were multiplied by their LD-based phase signs to express changes in the same orientation as each cluster's mapped genotype. The **median** slope across eligible SNPs within each cluster was the primary evolutionary response; mean slopes and alternative SNP-coverage thresholds were examined as sensitivities. This yielded 675 clusters with finite common-frequency evolutionary responses and complete multivariate GWAS effects. We correlated cluster-level responses with directly mapped composite effects, full seven-trait effects reconstructed from component GWAS, the reduced SF–SB–area.F index, and individual-trait effects. We repeated descriptive analyses among clusters associated with the composite at GWAS false-discovery rates below 0.05 and 0.01; these ascertainment-defined subsets were not treated as independent validation.

To assess uncertainty from genomic dependence, we resampled contiguous 2-Mb genomic blocks (1,500 replicates in the initial analysis) and obtained percentile intervals for correlations and for the full-minus-reduced contrast. We also calculated leave-one-chromosome-out predictions: on each fold, a linear regression of the cluster's evolutionary response on its GWAS predictor was fitted on five chromosomes and evaluated on the sixth; predictive \(R^2\) was calculated from pooled held-out predictions. An SB-only predictor served as a single-trait benchmark. The final targeted analysis (script 15) additionally provides an area.F-only benchmark, block-resampled contrasts in held-out squared error, and analyses excluding chromosome IV from evaluation. These are genomic robustness checks, not independent experimental replication. Exploratory chromosome-restricted permutation tests and alternative gradient directions were used only as sensitivity analyses.

Two candidate variants independently reported to have increased to approximately 30% frequency in two evolving populations and associated with SB were considered potential sources of local deviation from predictions based on ancestral segregating variation. **[AUTHOR: supply their genome-assembly-matched coordinates, allele identities, read counts/frequencies at each sampled generation, and the evidence supporting their origin.]** When these data are available, script 15 compares prediction errors inside and outside prespecified 0.25-, 0.5-, 1-, and 2-Mb windows around the variants and contrasts the effect of omitting these windows with omitting randomly positioned windows on chromosome IV. ## Results

### Fitness-related multivariate GWAS effects are associated with evolutionary allele-frequency change

The selection gradients estimated from relative fitness and the seven RIL phenotypes differed in strength and statistical support. SF had a negative point estimate and SB and area.F positive point estimates, but **only the area.F gradient excluded zero at the 95% confidence level** (Fig. XA). The other traits' estimated gradients were retained in the multivariate composite rather than equated to precisely zero. These estimates were not directly comparable in significance to the genetic or retrospective selection gradients reported by Mallard et al. (2023), because the methods estimate different quantities.

After correction of Pool-seq sample-to-generation alignment, the predicted multivariate genetic effect was positively associated with evolutionary allele-frequency change. Among the 675 R5 clusters with common-frequency sequencing information, the correlation was \(r=0.224\) for the independently mapped composite (95% genomic-block bootstrap interval 0.155–0.297) and \(r=0.246\) for the full index reconstructed from the seven trait GWAS effects (0.174–0.319; Fig. XB). Their associated proportions of response variance were approximately 5.0% and 6.1%, respectively, emphasizing that the correspondence was detectable but modest. Mapped and reconstructed composite GWAS effects were themselves strongly correlated (\(r=0.988\)). Among the 288 clusters significantly associated with the mapped composite at GWAS \(q<0.05\), the mapped-composite correlation with evolutionary response was \(r=0.288\); this ascertainment-defined result is presented as a sensitivity analysis rather than independent validation.

### The full index improves prediction modestly, but individual traits remain competitive

The reduced index containing SF, SB, and area.F was also positively associated with evolutionary response (\(r=0.212\), genomic-block bootstrap interval 0.143–0.281). The correlation of the reconstructed full index exceeded that of the reduced index by 0.034 (95% block-bootstrap interval 0.017–0.053), whereas the improvement of the **directly mapped** composite was smaller (difference 0.012, interval −0.006 to 0.032). Thus, the improvement depends to some degree on whether the full genetic effect is mapped directly or reconstructed from component-trait effects; the two approaches should not be presented as independent confirmations of seven-trait superiority.

Chromosome-held-out prediction supported a small gain from the reconstructed multivariate index. Across all 675 held-out cluster predictions, predictive \(R^2\) was 0.051 for the reconstructed full index, 0.040 for the mapped composite, and 0.035 for the reduced three-trait index (Fig. XC). SB alone achieved \(R^2=0.054\), comparable to the full index. Consequently, the results support partial predictability of evolutionary response from multivariate genetic effects, but not a general claim that the biologically weighted seven-trait predictor outperforms the best individual trait.

### Chromosome IV differs from the other chromosomes

The reconstructed full index gave lower held-out mean squared error than the reduced index on five of six chromosomes. Chromosome IV, containing 106 eligible clusters, was the sole exception: the full-minus-reduced reduction in mean squared error was approximately \(-3.53\times10^{-9}\) allele-frequency-slope-squared units, compared with positive values on chromosomes I, II, III, V and X (Fig. XD). This exception is small and does not by itself establish a significant chromosome-specific difference.

Two candidate variants on chromosome IV have been reported to rise from undetectable ancestral frequency to approximately 0.30 in two replicate populations and to show strong associations with SB. **[AUTHOR: cite the independent locus-level sequencing and genotype–phenotype evidence here.]** Such variants could contribute to evolutionary change not captured by GWAS of segregating ancestral variants, particularly through linkage with nearby polymorphisms. Nevertheless, an allele not observed at generation zero is not necessarily a mutation that arose *during* the selection experiment: low-frequency ancestral alleles and mutations arising during earlier laboratory maintenance remain alternatives.
